You can solve a cube with your eyes open. Congratulations, so can a lot of people with a YouTube tab. You turn, you squint, you turn again. A blindfolded solve takes the squinting away, but only after one long look, as long as you need. And you'll need a while.

A blindfolded solve has two halves. Try to keep both in your head.

## Half one: memorising

You look at the scrambled cube and work out, piece by piece, where everything goes. You don't turn it, even though your hands are already twitching. You turn what you see into something you can remember: a short list of letters, then words or pictures for those letters. Yes, you'll have to remember things. On purpose.

<Cube setup="U2 R B2 R'" label="A scrambled cube, the way you first see it" />

At the start this takes minutes. Plural. Nobody's timing you here, which is lucky.

## Half two: executing

Then the blindfold goes on and you solve from memory. You can't check anything, so your usual method is useless, since it relies entirely on seeing what you just did wrong.

Instead you solve **one piece at a time**. There's always a fixed starting spot, called the **buffer**. You move the piece in the buffer to where it belongs, with a short sequence that leaves everything else exactly as it was. Then you do the same for whatever piece landed in the buffer. Again and again, until every piece is home, or you are.

Here's one of those moves. Only the pieces it touches are lit. Press play and watch the rest of the cube stay put, which is more discipline than your last solve had.

<OpShot pieces="corners" target="UFR" />

That sequence never changes. What changes is a few moves before it, which bring the right spot into place, and the same moves undone afterwards. Nothing else is disturbed, so you can do it blind. You only need your memory and a sequence you trust. Pick one of those to work on.

## Why this is learnable

It sounds like a party trick for show-offs. It's actually a small set of ideas used in a fixed order:

1. Name every sticker with a letter, so a position becomes something you can say.
2. Follow pieces from the buffer, writing down letters as you go. That's tracing.
3. Turn the letters into words, so a long list becomes a short story.
4. Learn one sequence for corners and one for edges, plus the setup moves that feed them.
5. Handle the one special case, called parity, where the count comes out odd.

The path goes in exactly that order. By lesson 15 you'll do a whole solve, with the site guiding every step. Holding your hand, basically.

<Note>You don't need a fast solve, a special cube or a good memory to start. You need a cube you can already solve with your eyes open. You have that. No more excuses.</Note>
