Everything in a beginner blindfolded solve comes back to one spot on the cube for each kind of piece. That spot is the **buffer**.

## The buffers on this path

This path starts with a method called Old Pochmann. It uses:

- for corners, the buffer <Buffer method="op" pieces="corners" />, the top-back-left corner;
- for edges, the buffer <Buffer method="op" pieces="edges" />, the top-right edge.

Those are the usual buffers for Old Pochmann. If you've set different ones, every example here follows your settings.

## Shooting a target

Look at the sticker sitting in the corner buffer. It belongs somewhere; its home has a letter. That letter is a **target**. Solving it is called **shooting** to that target, and it always has three parts:

1. **Setup:** a few moves that bring the target's place to one fixed spot, called the swap spot, without touching the buffer.
2. **Swap:** one fixed sequence that exchanges the buffer with the swap spot.
3. **Undo:** the setup moves backwards, so everything the setup moved goes back.

The corner swap is always the same sequence: <SwapAlg pieces="corners" />. Only the setup changes from target to target. Here's one target shot, with the buffer and target lit:

<OpShot pieces="corners" target="UFR" />

After the undo, the piece that was in the buffer is home, and the piece that was sitting at the target is now in the buffer. Its sticker gives you the next target. That's the loop the whole solve is made of.

## Why nothing else breaks

The swap does move two other pieces as a side effect. For the corner swap it's two top-layer edges, and they swap back the next time you use it. That's why setups must never move the buffer or those side-effect pieces, and why some moves are off limits in setups. You'll see exactly which ones, and what goes wrong, in the Old Pochmann corners lesson.

<Note>You don't need to memorise the swap yet. First learn to read targets: that's tracing, next.</Note>
