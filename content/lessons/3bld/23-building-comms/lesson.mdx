The site gives you a verified comm for every case, but many solvers end up preferring their own. Building a comm is a short routine, and once you can do it no case is a mystery.

## The routine

For the buffer and two targets:

1. **Find an interchange.** Look for a layer, a face or a slice, that holds two of the three pieces. A single turn of it is the interchange.
2. **Find an insertion.** The third piece is outside that layer. Look for a short sequence, often three moves, that takes it into the layer and back out, touching only one of the layer's slots.
3. **Put them together** as [insertion, interchange] or [interchange, insertion].
4. **Check the direction.** If the pieces cycle the wrong way round, swap the two parts: [B, A] is the reverse of [A, B].
5. **No interchange or insertion fits?** Add a setup that moves one piece so they do, and write the whole thing as [setup: [A, B]].

## Checking your work

The commutator sandbox in Practice takes any comm you type, expands it, counts its moves and tells you exactly which pieces it moves. It only calls something a 3-cycle when the engine has checked it. Use it for every comm you build.

## Choosing between comms

When a case has more than one comm, prefer:

- **fewer moves after cancellation,** since moves at the joins often cancel;
- **no regrips,** so your hands stay in one position throughout;
- **moves you're fast at,** even if the count is one higher;
- **a shape you already know** from a neighbouring case, since it's easier to recall.

In the 3-style trainer you can save your own comm for any case. It's checked before it's saved, and yours is shown first from then on.
