Old Pochmann and M2 solve one target at a time. A **commutator** solves two at once: it moves three pieces round a cycle, the buffer and two targets, and leaves every other piece where it was. That's the whole of 3-style.

## The notation

A commutator is written **[A, B]**, where A and B are short move sequences. It means:

1. do A;
2. do B;
3. undo A;
4. undo B.

So [R, U] is R U R' U'. Nothing more to it.

A **conjugate** is written **[S: X]** and means S, then X, then undo S. It's how you set pieces up so that a commutator can reach them, exactly the way Old Pochmann's setups work.

## Watch one, part by part

Step through this commutator. The three pieces it cycles are lit the whole time; watch everything else come back.

<CommParts comm="[B, L F' L']" />

After A and B, the cube looks badly scrambled. Undoing A puts most of it back, and undoing B finishes the job, except for three pieces.

## Insertion and interchange

The two parts of a commutator like this have jobs:

- The **interchange** is a single turn of a layer that holds two of the three pieces. Here it's B.
- The **insertion** is a short sequence that takes the third piece into that layer and back out, touching only one slot of the layer. Here it's L F' L'.

Which one goes first decides which way round the three pieces cycle. Swapping them, [L F' L', B], cycles the same three pieces the other way.

## Why only three pieces move

A and B each move plenty of pieces, and the undos put almost all of them back. Trouble can only happen where A and B overlap. When they share exactly one slot, as an insertion and an interchange do, what's left over is always a cycle of exactly three pieces.
