Tracing gives you a list like I Q C S for the corners. Ten or twenty letters in a row are hard to hold; a handful of pictures are easy. So the letters are grouped in twos, and every two-letter group gets an image.

## Pairs

Read the list two letters at a time, straight through. Cycle breaks and twists don't change anything here: the pairs run right across them.

<TraceWalk scramble="U2 R B2 R'" pieces="corners" mode="show" shows="no-breaks" label="Tracing corners into a memo to split into pairs" />

That memo is I Q, C S: two pairs.

## Images

For each pair, think of **the first word or picture that comes to mind**. It can be a thing, a person, a place, an in-joke. IQ might be an intelligence test; CS might be a computer science lecture hall. It doesn't have to start with those letters, or be spelled with them. It has to be vivid and yours.

Then the memo isn't four letters, it's two pictures: a test paper, a lecture hall. Make them interact if you like, the test paper blowing into the lecture hall. A tiny story is easier to recall than a list.

Over time you'll build an image for every pair. There are 24 × 24 = 576 cells, and you don't need them all on day one: pairs you actually meet in traces come first. The **letter pair library** in Practice keeps yours, and reviews them before you forget.

## A letter left over

If a list has an odd number of targets, one letter is left alone at the end. The simplest habit is to **double it**: a lone U becomes the pair UU, with its own image. That's what this site does by default.

<TraceWalk scramble="D B2 D2" pieces="corners" mode="show" shows="no-breaks" label="An odd-length corner memo, with a letter left over" />

That memo is V B, W X, and a lone U, remembered as UU.

<Note>An odd number of targets also means parity, which changes how you solve, not how you memorise. That's lesson 14.</Note>
