**Recognition does not change.** A one-handed solver sees the same cross, the same F2L pairs, the same OLL and PLL pictures as a two-handed one, with yellow on top and white on the bottom. What changes is **how the turns are made** with one hand: which turns are comfortable, which need a regrip, and which are awkward enough to replace.

Do not assume that the best two-handed algorithm is the best one-handed algorithm. They are different problems.

## F2L with one hand

F2L is mostly R and U turns, which suit one hand. The advice from the last lesson matters even more here: choose the pair that needs the fewest regrips, keep the cube from rotating, and prefer solutions that keep your grip where it is. The F2L reference on this site has one solution per case; it does not have separate one-handed rows, and it says so. Use it to compare, and keep your own notes on what your hand finds smooth.

## Last layer: where the algorithms differ

For OLL and PLL the site has **dedicated one-handed algorithms** next to the two-handed ones, supplied and checked against the cube engine. Many are different:

- Middle-slice turns (M) need a full-hand movement one-handed. The two-handed **H, Ua, Ub and Z** permutations use them, and the one-handed versions are written with R, L and U turns instead.
- Wide turns and rotations are placed differently, so the algorithm runs on the faces that are comfortable for your hand.
- A few algorithms, such as **T** and the four **G** perms, are the same in both styles because they already suit one hand.

## Recognise first, then execute

Run recognition and execution as two skills. First name the case without turning. Then execute the algorithm slowly, marking each regrip you make. Only combine the two when each part is reliable. The recognition practice has a one-handed setting, so the algorithm that plays after your answer is the one-handed version.
